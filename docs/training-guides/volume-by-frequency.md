# Weekly volume from training frequency (rule 1 rewrite)

*Training-researcher survey for Ben. Sources checked 2026-10-09; fact-checked the same day (see "Fact-check corrections" at the end). Not medical advice. Builds on [evidence-core.md](evidence-core.md) (E1, E2, E5, E10–E13), [concurrent-training.md](concurrent-training.md) (C1–C5), [goal-adaptive.md](goal-adaptive.md) (GA) and [programmes.md](programmes.md). Replaces the fixed "start near 10" in shortlist rule 1, as Ben asked.*

**Summary**
1. More weekly hard sets per muscle give more growth, with diminishing returns (Strong). In one meta-regression, about 4 sets a week is the smallest detectable dose, 5–10 is the most efficient range and 11–18 still pays (Moderate). Strength gains per extra set drop sharply after about 3–4 sets of the lift a week (Moderate).
2. Frequency mainly decides how the sets are spread (Strong). Within one session, extra sets stop adding detectable growth at about 8 direct (≈ 11 fractional) sets, and at about 2 sets of the lift for strength. That figure comes from one preprint whose authors call it "not an upper limit" (Weak). So Ben's days and session length set a realistic weekly ceiling.
3. Proposed start for each muscle: sessions that train it × the sets that fit in each session, or Ben's own logged median when he has ≥ 4 weeks of logs. His goal sets the range (Practice, built on the above).
4. Sets grow by 1–2 only when Ben asks or accepts an offer, and only while performance holds and time allows. Otherwise load or reps go up. Evidence that adding sets helps growth is Weak; for strength it is Moderate. In a short week, keep loads, cut sets and don't make them up later.
5. A lasting change in frequency is asked about first, then proposed with Apply/Undo. Ben's stated days, session length and goal never change silently.

### Guardrail notes (CLAUDE.md, ADR 0006)

- **No guardrail is overridden.** These rules change sets and plans, not load limits. Any exercise or load change still goes through:
  - achievable-load snapping (round down after an overshoot)
  - equipment min/max
  - the pain block
  - completed-set immutability
  - version checks

  Every applied change stores its reason and the evidence used (ADR 0006).
- **Rule 3 / ADR 0006: conflict to flag (V5 and V7).** The ADR allows only these coach-raised prompts: RPE ≥ 9.5, reps short by ≥ 2, and RPE ≤ 6. So a fixed "review volume every 2 weeks" (V5) or a frequency-drift question (V7) would be a new unprompted prompt. Suggested reading: they appear only in one of these places:
  - when Ben asks ("plan next week", "should I add sets?")
  - in a conversation he starts
  - as a single line in a summary or plan screen he opens ("Chest has held for 2 weeks — want me to propose +2 sets?")

  Nothing applies until he accepts. The alternative is to add them to ADR 0006 as deterministic triggers. **Ben to decide.**
- **Rule 2:** any change to future sessions or templates is a proposal with before/after, scope, Apply and Undo. Today's sets change only when Ben asks (applied with Undo) or after a trigger offer that he accepts.
- **Rule 6 (data honesty):**
  - Weekly set counts come from logs only. Sets without RPE (e.g. FitNotes imports) are counted as hard and labelled "approx.".
  - Endurance adjustments in V4 apply only when the session is planned, imported or reported by Ben.
  - No recovery scores are inferred, and minutes per set come from Ben's own timestamps.
- **Rule 9 (pain):**
  - Sets are never added to a group whose movement has an active pain flag.
  - The coach doesn't shift volume onto other exercises to "work around" pain unless Ben asks.
  - It doesn't diagnose; it suggests seeing a professional.
- **Rule 10:** the bands, caps and maths here are **Coaching knowledge**. Ben's days a week, session length, goal, priority groups, minutes per set and habitual medians are **Your data**, with provenance and dates. A new frequency is saved only after Ben confirms it.
- **No-AI fallback (ADR 0006 and its approved 2026-10-09 amendment):**
  - The built-in rule progresses load, or reps when the smallest achievable increment is too large or hits a machine's max.
  - It may add a set only in that same equipment case, and only within V2 and V4.
  - It doesn't otherwise change weekly sets or apply V5. It may show weekly set counts.

## Details

Labels used below: **Fact** = what a source documents. **Opinion** = expert or coach view. **Inference** = my reasoning.

### 1. Weekly volume: how much is enough

**Fact**
- **Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson & Zourdos**, *Sports Medicine* 2026;56(2):481–505, published online 4 Dec 2025 (PMID 41343037), doi [10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w). The journal page redirected to a login, so the numbers below were read from the open preprint [SRXIV.460](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460) (v2, 2024-10-04). The journal abstract (via Europe PMC) matches the preprint's direction of findings.
  - 67 studies, 2,058 participants (mean age 25; mean intervention 10.4 weeks). Studies whose data were called into question (Vigotsky et al.) were excluded.
  - Hypertrophy rises with weekly *fractional* sets, with diminishing returns and no clear plateau. The authors caution that few studies explored ~25+ fractional sets a week.
  - Preprint "efficiency tiers" (Table 2A; smallest detectable effect 2.05%):
    - minimum effective dose **4** sets a week
    - **5–10** higher efficiency
    - **11–18** intermediate
    - **19–29** lower
    - **30–42** lowest
    - 43+ unclear
  - Strength (Table 2B; smallest detectable effect 3.96%): **1** set a week is detectable, increments are detectable up to about **3–4**, and at **5+** "additional weekly sets do not consistently enhance strength gains". The authors add two caveats:
    - 3.96% may be larger than what matters in practice.
    - Learning a new test may inflate low-volume effects. In trained powerlifters, 1–3 direct sets a week weren't enough, but 3–9 were.
  - **Not verified:** the per-session preprint (Remmert 2025, below) quotes Pelland as finding "no detectable superiority" beyond ~**31** fractional sets a week for hypertrophy and ~**3** for strength, and a strength-frequency plateau at ~3 sessions a week. These figures are not in the open preprint and may come from the revised journal version.
  - Conflict of interest: the authors declare they are "coaches and writers in the fitness industry".
- **Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017** (J Sports Sci 35(11):1073–82; 15 studies, 34 groups; PMID 27433992): each extra weekly set was associated with about 0.37% more growth. Comparing < 5, 5–9 and 10+ sets showed only a non-significant trend (p = 0.074). doi [10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197). *(Earlier versions of this file and evidence-core.md linked PMC6303131, which is a different paper; see below.)*
- **Schoenfeld et al. 2019** (MSSE; 34 trained men; 1, 3 or 5 sets per exercise, 3×/week, 8 weeks): strength gains were similar across groups, and growth was greater with more volume at 3 of 4 sites. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6303131/>
- **Baz-Valle et al. 2022** (J Hum Kinet; 7 RCTs, men trained ≥ 1 year): 12–20 vs > 20 weekly sets made no difference for quads (p = 0.19) or biceps (p = 0.59); triceps favoured > 20 (p = 0.01). The authors suggest 12–20 as a standard for trained young men. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877/>
- **ACSM 2026 position stand** (MSSE; an overview of reviews). Free infographic: "Train all major muscle groups at least 2 days per week and build gradually over time". For hypertrophy, "higher weekly volume (~10 sets per muscle group)"; for strength, 80% 1RM "for 2–3 sets per exercise". <https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf>. The full text is paywalled.

**Inference:** 10 is not a magic starting number; it is roughly where the "efficient" tier ends, and 6–8 sets are still productive. For a time-limited lifter, the evidence supports starting wherever his schedule lands in the 5–18 range and growing only if time and recovery allow.

### 2. The per-session ceiling

**Fact**
- **Remmert, Pelland, Robinson, Hinson & Zourdos 2025**, SportRxiv preprint (posted 2025-04-02, **not peer reviewed**). <https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537>
  - Data: 35 studies (1,032 participants) for hypertrophy; 66 studies (2,020 participants) for strength.
  - Per-session volume had a positive dose-response for both outcomes, with diminishing returns that were stronger for strength.
  - The "point of undetectable outcome superiority" (PUOS) is where more sets no longer have a > 50% chance of a detectable extra gain.
    - Hypertrophy: **11 fractional** sets per session ≈ **8.2 direct** ≈ 13.8 total.
    - Strength: **2 direct** sets ≈ 3.5 fractional ≈ 5 total.
    - The authors say these "are not upper limits" and that the thresholds were "determined arbitrarily".
  - Data thin out at high per-session volumes: the mean was 5.95 ± 4.49 fractional sets, and 12 studies reached ≥ 10 sets per session.
- **Krieger's unpublished 2017 meta-regression**, as described in Remmert 2025: 15 studies, counting every set as 1 ("total"). It found an apparent break at about **6–8 sets per session** when rest was ≥ 2 min, with only 2 studies at ≥ 11 sets per session.

**Inference:** a single session can't usefully absorb a missed session's volume. Above ~8 direct sets per muscle, extra sets probably add more fatigue and time than growth. That's why session count and length, not a fixed weekly number, set Ben's realistic weekly volume. The cap of 8 direct sets is conservative against Remmert and at the top of Krieger's range.

### 3. Frequency: a way to distribute volume

**Fact**
- **Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019** (J Sports Sci 37(11):1286–95; 25 studies): with volume equated, frequency made no meaningful difference to hypertrophy. Without equating, higher frequency helped modestly, because it added volume. doi [10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906)
- **Pelland 2025 (preprint figures):**
  - Hypertrophy: the frequency effect was small and uncertain (0.32% per session, 95% CrI −0.14 to 0.82).
  - Strength: positive (3.27% per session, CrI 2.74–3.84). The biggest step was from 1 to 2 sessions a week (≈ 12.7% → 17.3%), with steep diminishing returns after that.
- **Häkkinen et al. 2003** (Eur J Appl Physiol; 27 men aged ~37; 21 weeks; 2 strength + 2 endurance days a week): 1RM and muscle size grew the same as with strength-only training (1RM +22% vs +21%), but rate of force development didn't improve. doi [10.1007/s00421-002-0751-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-002-0751-9)

**Inference:** for muscle, frequency matters mainly because it raises the weekly sets that fit under the per-session cap and the time budget. For strength, practising each main lift 2× a week is the main win, and a 3rd exposure is a small extra.

### 4. Strength: the dose for the lift itself

**Fact**
- **Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021** (Front Sports Act Living). This is a mixed-methods paper combining expert interviews (n = 28), surveys and two small training studies (n = 16 and n = 9). Its minimum effective dose is ≈ **3–6 working sets of 1–5 reps per lift per week**, over 1–3 sessions, at > 80% 1RM and RPE 7.5–9.5. Singles alone were less likely to give meaningful gains; adding 2–3 back-off sets helped. doi [10.3389/fspor.2021.713655](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655). Co-authors include coaches with commercial businesses (e.g. Nuckols, Stronger by Science).
- **Androulakis-Korakakis, Fisher & Steele 2020** (Sports Med; 6 studies, 5 meta-analysed, trained men): one hard set of 6–12 reps at ~70–85%, 2–3× a week, gave "suboptimal, yet significant" squat and bench 1RM gains. doi [10.1007/s40279-019-01236-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01236-0)
- **Aube et al. 2022** (JSCR; 35 trained lifters; 8 weeks; 2×/week): 12, 18 and 24 weekly lower-body sets. Quad thickness didn't differ. Squat 1RM gain trended best at 18 sets (+16.2%) and worst at 24 (+5.4%; p = 0.052). doi [10.1519/JSC.0000000000003524](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003524)
- **Rhea et al. 2003** (MSSE; 140 studies; older and criticised): for strength, untrained people did best training each muscle ~3 days a week and trained people ~2 days, both with ~4 sets per muscle group. doi [10.1249/01.MSS.0000053727.63505.D4](https://doi.org/10.1249/01.MSS.0000053727.63505.D4)

**Inference:** for a strength goal, 3–6 heavy sets per main lift a week is enough. That band combines Pelland's strength tiers, Remmert's ~2 sets per session and Androulakis-Korakakis. Extra days should go to accessories, muscle work or endurance, not more main-lift sets.

### 5. Minimum and maintenance doses

**Fact**
- **Iversen, Norum, Schoenfeld & Fimland 2021** (Sports Med; a narrative review for people short of time):
  - "at least 4 weekly sets per muscle", at 6–15RM, or 15–40 reps if taken to failure
  - weekly volume matters more than frequency
  - supersets, drop sets and rest-pause roughly halve session time
  - "we do not advise these methods for heavy compound, free-weight exercises such as the squat and bench press"

  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772/>
- **Bickel, Cross & Bamman 2011** (MSSE RCT; 70 adults). After 16 weeks of training 3×/week, 32 weeks at **⅓** or even **1/9** of the dose kept muscle size in 20–35-year-olds but not in 60–75-year-olds. Strength was largely kept, even in the group that stopped training, so strength retention says little about the dose needed. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131862/>
- **Spiering, Mujika, Sharp & Foulis 2021** (JSCR narrative review): younger adults kept strength and size for up to 32 weeks with **1 session a week and 1 set per exercise, as long as relative load stays the same**. Older adults may need 2 sessions a week and 2–3 sets per exercise. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/>
- **RP (opinion, commercial: sells an app and books; 2017 page):**
  - Maintenance volume is "around 6 working sets per muscle group per week", with each muscle trained ≥ 2×.
  - For beginners, minimum effective volume "sits close to" maintenance.

  <https://rpstrength.com/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth/>

### 6. Adding volume over time

**Fact**
- **Enes, De Souza & Souza-Junior 2024** (MSSE; 31 trained men; 12 weeks; legs 2×/week). Weekly sets were kept constant or raised by +4 or +6 every 2 weeks. Squat 1RM gain ranked 6-set > 4-set > constant. Muscle size didn't differ significantly (p ≈ 0.07), with signs of a plateau. doi [10.1249/MSS.0000000000003317](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003317)
- **Enes et al. 2025** (J Sports Sci; 30 trained women). 22 sets constant vs +2 or +4 sets every 2 weeks: both progressions gave more leg-press 1RM. Only +4 beat constant on one size measure (VL-CSA); summed thickness didn't differ. doi [10.1080/02640414.2025.2459003](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2025.2459003)
- **Camargo et al. 2026** (J Appl Physiol 141(2):477–93, online 16 Jul 2026; 25 trained; 8 weeks; one leg per condition). A +120% jump over habitual volume gave the same growth as +20%: no harm, no extra benefit. No strength outcome is reported in the abstract. doi [10.1152/japplphysiol.00284.2026](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00284.2026)
- **RP (opinion):** adds sets weekly (example 12 → 14 → 16 → 18 → 20, then a deload to 6). Soreness-recovery and performance scores decide the step: +2–3 sets, +1, hold, or a recovery session/deload. (Link above.)

**Inference:**
- Small steps (+1–2 sets, ≈ 10–20%) are enough, and big jumps buy nothing extra.
- The trials started from ~22 sets and added 2–6 every 2 weeks, so they say little about the right step size at 6–15 sets; the +1–2 step is RP-style practice.
- Progressive set increases helped strength more consistently than size. That is why V5 is graded Weak for growth.

### 7. Starting from Ben's own history

**Fact**
- **Scarpelli et al. 2022** (JSCR; 16 trained; within-subject). The leg trained at 1.2× each person's own logged weekly sets grew more than the leg on a standard 22 sets (effect size 0.75), and more participants exceeded measurement error. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32108724/>
- **Hammarström et al. 2020** (J Physiol; 34 untrained; 3 sets vs 1 set per exercise). Higher volume was better on average (size +5.2% vs +3.7%), but only 13 of 34 people clearly benefited for size and 16 of 34 for strength. doi [10.1113/JP278455](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP278455)

**Inference:** individuals vary a lot, so Ben's own logged volume (from FitNotes import and app logs) is a better anchor than any population number. The evidence for "×1.2 of habitual" comes from one small trial (Weak).

### 8. Time per set and supersets

**Fact**
- Rest: 2–3 min (up to 5) on heavy compounds, 1–2 min on isolation work (E10; [Singer 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/text)). Iversen 2021: ≥ 2 min for trained lifters.
- **Zhang et al. 2025** (Sports Med meta-analysis; 19 studies, 313 participants; mostly young trained men): <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12011898/>
  - Supersets cut session time by about 37%, with similar volume load overall.
  - Agonist–antagonist pairs allowed *more* reps (2 studies). Same-muscle pairs reduced volume load (2 studies).
  - Long-term strength and size were similar, but only 3 long-term studies exist.
  - Perceived exertion and lactate were higher.

**Inference (the maths behind V2):**
- A heavy compound set ≈ 45 s of work + 2–3 min rest ≈ 3–3.75 min, plus changeover. An isolation set ≈ 40 s + 60–90 s ≈ 2–2.2 min.
- With the brief's rest presets (60 / 90 / 180 s) and a typical mix, 3 min per working set is a reasonable default, and ~2.5 min when accessories are supersetted. About 10 minutes go on warm-ups.
- The ÷ 3 default is optimistic for compound-heavy sessions with long rests and extra ramp-up sets. Hence ~4 min per heavy main-lift set, and calibration from Ben's own timestamps after 3 sessions.
- Check on the 3-day sketch below (squat/bench/RDL at 180 s rest, rows at 90 s, two superset pairs, 10 min warm-up plus ~2 min ramp-up per extra main lift): ≈ 62 min. That matches the 60-minute budget closely enough.

| Session length | Sets that fit, straight sets (÷ 3) | Sets that fit, accessories supersetted (÷ 2.5) |
|---|---|---|
| 30 min | 6 | 8 |
| 45 min | 11 | 14 |
| 60 min | 16 | 20 |
| 75 min | 21 | 26 |
| 90 min | 26 | 32 |

### 9. Short weeks, missed weeks and breaks

**Fact**
- **Ogasawara et al. 2013** (14 young men; bench press 3×/week): 3-week breaks every 6 weeks gave the same 24-week size and strength gains as continuous training. <https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-012-2511-9>
- **Coleman et al. 2024** (PeerJ; 39 trained; 9 weeks): a 1-week full break at week 5 didn't change lower-body hypertrophy, but lower-body strength was slightly worse (see E11). <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/>
- Bickel 2011 and Spiering 2021 (above): reduced volume with intensity kept preserves gains for months.

**Inference:** one or two light weeks don't need "making up". Cramming the missing sets into the remaining sessions would push them past the per-session cap, adding fatigue for little gain. Keeping loads (intensity) is the part that protects progress.

### 10. Events

See [goal-adaptive.md](goal-adaptive.md) (GA8, GA19, event table). **Fact:** **Bosquet et al. 2007** (MSSE meta-analysis; 27 studies of competitive athletes): the best taper was ~2 weeks, reducing volume exponentially by 41–60% while keeping intensity and frequency. doi [10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0). **Inference:** heavy lower-body work stays ≥ 48 h away from key sessions (shortlist rule 12, applied only when endurance training is in the plan).

### Where sources disagree

- **How high weekly volume can usefully go:** ACSM says ~10, Baz-Valle says 12–20, and Pelland finds no clear plateau, with few studies above ~25 sets.
  - Why they differ: ACSM summarises average adults, while Pelland models the dose-response across all data.
  - For Ben (limited time, other training), 6–18 is the practical range; above 20 only for one or two priority groups.
- **Per-session ceiling: 6–8 vs ~11 fractional.** These do **not** agree once put in the same units.
  - Krieger's 6–8 counted every set as 1 ("total") and had only 2 studies at ≥ 11 sets per session.
  - Remmert's equivalent in total sets is ~14, from 12 studies at ≥ 10 sets per session.
  - Remmert attributes the gap to counting method and more data.
  - The ~8-direct planning cap sits between them. Because both analyses are unpublished or unreviewed, the exact cap is graded Weak.
- **Frequency and strength:** Grgic 2018 found no volume-equated frequency effect (E12). Pelland 2025 found a positive effect using non-linear models and fractional counting. Both support "each main lift ≥ 2×/week" as a sensible default; the size of the effect is uncertain (Moderate).
- **Do more sets help strength?** Enes 2024/2025 found progressive set increases helped 1RM. Aube 2022 found a trend that 24 sets did worse than 18. **Inference:** modest increases help, while very high leg volume may blunt strength, especially alongside endurance work.

## The algorithm (for the coach)

**Inputs, all Your data:**
- **D:** lifting days in a usual week, as Ben states them.
- **T:** usual lifting minutes per session (per day if they differ).
- **Goal and priority groups.**
- **Experience:** new (< ~6 months of consistent training), intermediate (~6 months–3 years) or experienced (> 3 years).
- **Logs**, including FitNotes imports.
- **Planned, imported or reported endurance sessions.**
- **Active pain flags.**

1. **Budget per session:** C = ⌊(T − 10) ÷ 3⌋, or ⌊(T − 10) ÷ 2.5⌋ if Ben accepts supersets on accessories. For strength sessions, count ~4 min per heavy main-lift set. After 3 logged sessions, use his median minutes per working set.
2. **Split and exposures (F):** default split by D as in V3; Ben's own split overrides. Plan with "slots":
   - full body = 5 slots (chest, back, quads, hips, and shoulders or arms on alternate days)
   - upper = 4 slots (chest, back, shoulders, arms)
   - lower = 3 slots (quads, hips, calves/core)
   - push, pull or legs = 3 slots each

   "Hips" covers the hamstrings and glutes groups; "arms" covers biceps and triceps, usually supersetted. Calves, core and forearms are "if time remains" unless Ben makes them a priority. Ben can weight slots unequally (e.g. chest and back over arms).
3. **Share per slot:** s = min(8, ⌊C ÷ slots⌋). For a strength goal, the main lift takes 1–3 hard sets of that share.
4. **Start:**
   - **History** (≥ 4 logged weeks in the last 8): start = Ben's median weekly fractional sets for the group. For a muscle goal with stable performance, up to ×1.2. Cap at F × s; if his habit exceeds what fits, say so (see example C).
   - **No history:** start = F × s, capped at 8 / 12 / 14 for new / intermediate / experienced.
5. **Goal check (V4):** trim anything above the band. If the start is below the band floor because of time (not the new-lifter cap), tell Ben the trade-off and offer options. Don't cram.
6. **Progress (V5):** only when Ben asks or accepts an offer. Add +1–2 sets/week to a priority group, placed in the session where that group has the fewest sets. If no session has room, progress load/reps instead.
7. **Week-to-week (V6/V7):** short week → merge and keep loads. Long week → spread. Lasting change → ask once (GA16), then propose a rebuilt plan.

### A. Intermediate, muscle goal, 60-minute sessions, no history

Budget C = 16 sets per session with straight sets, or 20 with accessory supersets.

**Sketch for 3 days, one 60-minute session.** Squat 3, bench 3, row 3, Romanian deadlift 3, then lateral raise 2 supersetted with curls or pushdowns 2 → 16 sets (≈ 62 min; see §8). Sessions B and C rotate variations (e.g. front squat or leg press, incline press, pull-up). Weekly totals:
- Chest, back, quads and hamstrings: ≈ 9 sets each.
- Side delts: ≈ 6 direct sets.
- Biceps and triceps: ≈ 3 direct sets each, plus ≈ 4–5 from ½-credits on rows and presses.

### B. The same days, other goals

- **Strength (60 min):** each main lift (squat, bench, a hinge, overhead press, a row or pull-up) gets 3–6 hard sets a week at RPE 7–9, 1–6 reps.
  - 2 days: 2 exposures × 2–3 sets. The hinge is heavy once and a lighter variant once.
  - 3 days: heavy / medium / light, 1–2 sets each.
  - 4 days (upper/lower): 2 × 2–3.
  - 5–6 days: still 2–3 exposures and ≤ ~6 sets per lift. The extra days go to accessories, muscle work or endurance.
  - Other groups: 4–10.
  - Heavy sets take longer (3–5 min rest), so budget about 4 min per main-lift set.
- **General fitness (45 min → 11 sets):**
  - 2 days full body → 2 sets per slot → **4 a week**, which is the minimum effective dose.
  - 3 days → **6**.
  - 4 days upper/lower → 4–6.
- **Maintain** (e.g. a busy month): ⅓ of recent volume, floor ~4. Example: a recent chest median of 12 → 4 a week, at the same loads, 1–2 days a week, with ≥ 1 heavy set per main lift.
- **Alongside endurance** (planned, imported or reported): for example 3 lifting days plus 3 runs or rides. Lower-body groups get 4–8 a week as 2 heavy exposures, timed with shortlist rules 11–12; upper body follows the goal.

### C. Starting from history (muscle goal, 4 days × 60 min)

Hypothetical import medians over 8 weeks: chest 11, back 13, quads 7, hamstrings 5. Room from Table A: upper 8 (10 with supersets), lower 10 (12 with supersets).

- **Quads:** start 7–8. Room to grow to 10–12.
- **Hamstrings:** start 5–6. If they're a priority, grow towards 8–10.
- **Chest and back:** the habit exceeds the room. The coach says: "Your logs show about 11 chest and 13 back sets a week. Four 60-minute sessions fit about 8, or 10 if I superset your accessories. To keep about 12, I could superset accessories and make the upper days about 70 minutes, or give chest and back more of the upper-day sets than arms and shoulders. Which do you prefer?" Nothing changes until Ben picks one; it's a proposal.

  (The previous draft said 70 minutes alone was enough. With straight sets, 70 minutes gives ⌊60 ÷ 3⌋ = 20 sets ÷ 4 slots = 5 sets per session, which is still 10 a week.)

### D. Frequency changes

- **Short week.** Plan: upper/lower ×2 (upper 8, lower 10). Ben: "Only 2 days this week." The coach offers 2 full-body sessions of 60 min:
  - Each session: squat 3, bench 3, row or pull-up 3, RDL 3, overhead press 2, arms superset 2.
  - Weekly result: ≈ 6 sets per major group, about 60–75% of plan (6 of 8 upper, 6 of 10 lower). Every main lift is done at its planned load.
  - It doesn't pack 8–10 sets per group into each session, and doesn't add extra next week.
- **Long week.** Plan: full body ×3 (9 a week). Ben: "I can do 5 days this week." Two options:
  - (a) The same 9 sets per group spread over 5 shorter (~35–40 min) sessions.
  - (b) The 3 normal sessions plus 2 short ones adding 1–2 sets to priority groups (≤ +20%).

  Not 15 a week.
- **Lasting change.** Ben stated 4 days, but his logs show 2, 2, 3, 2 over 4 weeks. The coach asks once, in a summary or chat Ben opens: "You've been training about 2 days a week lately. Want me to rebuild the plan for 2 days?" If Ben agrees:
  - The coach proposes full body ×2 (6–8 per group, main lifts every session) with before/after.
  - Ben's stated frequency is updated (with provenance) only after he confirms.

  If he says no, the coach doesn't ask again for ≥ 4 weeks unless a new signal appears (GA16).
- **Uneven session lengths.** Tue 45 min, Thu 45 min, Sat 90 min, all full body. Budgets 11 / 11 / 26 → shares 2 / 2 / 5 → ≈ 9 sets per group a week. Saturday carries the priority work.
