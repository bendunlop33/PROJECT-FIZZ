# Sport-support accessories: what to add for running (and cycling, swimming, team sports)

**Summary (plain words, for Ben)**
1. The best-supported thing lifting does for running is what you already do: **heavy compound lifts** (≥ ~80% 1RM) improve running economy, and plyometrics help a bit more. Grade: Moderate. No accessory has good evidence that it **prevents** running injuries; a 2024 meta-analysis of runner programmes found no overall effect.
2. Worth adding when you run: **calf raises, bent-knee and straight-knee** (the calves and soleus carry big loads when running; weak calves go with Achilles problems), **hip abduction**, and swapping one leg exercise for a **single-leg** one. About 6–8 sets a week across both leg days, after the main lifts. Grade: Weak to Moderate, depending on the claim.
3. The app suggests them **in the plan proposal when it builds a block and you run** (the endurance switch is on), or when you ask, or once when your other training changes. Skip means not again that block. "Not for me" means never, unless you ask. They're never added on their own, and never mid-block.
4. Optional, only when you want them: plyometrics (pogo jumps) if running speed matters, Nordics, adduction (the coach's suggestion; fine, but no running evidence), tibialis raises. Not suggested at all: stretching or foam rolling to prevent injury, "fixing imbalances", rehab for a sore knee or Achilles. Pain goes to the pain hold and "see a professional".
5. Wording stays honest: "common for runners", "supports calf strength", never "prevents injury".

## Details

### 1. Prevention: does strength work stop running injuries?

**Documented fact**
- **Wu et al. 2024** (*Sports Med*; 9 RCTs, 1,904 endurance runners; authors include R. Blagrove, an academic who also coaches runners): exercise-based programmes didn't reduce running-related injury risk or rate overall: *"Exercise-based interventions do not appear to reduce the risk and rate of running-related injuries."* A post hoc pool of 3 small supervised trials did show a reduction, which the authors link to adherence. Only 4 trials used strength exercises, mostly low load and low volume; none tested traditional heavy strength training. 7 of 9 trials were rated high risk of bias. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11127851/>
- **Toresdahl et al. 2020** (*Sports Health*; RCT, 720 first-time NYC marathoners): a self-directed 10-minute routine (quads, hip abductors, core), 3×/week, didn't change overuse injury causing non-completion (7.1% vs 7.3%) or finish time. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31642726/>
- **Lauersen et al. 2014** (*BJSM*; 25 RCTs, 26,610 participants, mostly team sport and military): strength training cut sports injuries to about a third (RR 0.315); stretching showed no effect (RR 0.963). **Lauersen 2018** (*BJSM*): strength-training benefits looked dose-dependent (more volume and intensity, more reduction). <https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/39522>; 2018 record <https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/24/1557.info> (abstract-level only).

**Why they disagree.** Lauersen pooled sports with sudden, high-force injuries (football, handball, military) and included targeted programmes (Nordics, balance). Running injuries are mostly overuse and linked to load changes, and the runner trials used light, unsupervised routines with poor adherence. **Inference:** there's no evidence against Ben's runner accessories, but no good evidence that they prevent running injury either. So SA10 bans the claim, and SA3 rests on calf strength, rehab evidence and running economy instead.

### 2. Performance: running economy and speed

- **Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024** (*Sports Med*; middle and long-distance runners): high-load (≥ 80% 1RM) training improved running economy (ES −0.27, moderate certainty), as did plyometrics (−0.31, moderate, mainly at ≤ 12 km/h) and combined methods (−0.43, low). Submaximal (40–79%) and isometric training didn't. <https://repository.essex.ac.uk/37272/>
- **Blagrove et al. 2018** (*Sports Med*; 24 studies): running economy improved 2–8% in most studies, along with time-trial (1.5–10 km) and sprint performance; VO2max and body composition were unchanged. The authors suggest 2–3 strength sessions a week across several modalities. <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5889786/>
- **Barrio et al. 2023** (*Kinesiology*; 10 studies in the meta-analysis): plyometrics alone had a trivial effect on economy (ES 0.19), plyometrics + resistance training a large one (1.34). Effects were bigger with > 15 sessions, > 7 weeks and > 2 days a week (small subgroups). <https://hrcak.srce.hr/ojs/index.php/kinesiology/article/view/24698>
- **Rønnestad & Mujika 2014** (*Scand J Med Sci Sports*, review): running economy improves with heavy or explosive strength work, and there are no reports of harm to running or cycling economy. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23914932/> (journal version paid: see the README list).

**Inference for Ben:** his heavy squats, deadlifts and presses already deliver the best-supported running benefit. That's why SA3 keeps main lifts as planned and treats plyometrics as an option: they only clearly add on top of lifting, and mainly for speed or economy goals.

### 3. Calves and soleus

- **Dorn, Schache & Pandy 2012** (*J Exp Biol*; modelling, n = 9): up to about 7 m/s, soleus and gastrocnemius contribute most to vertical support in running. Mechanistic (Weak). Body-weight multiples quoted on blogs weren't confirmed in the abstract and aren't used here. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22573774/>
- **van der Vlist et al. 2019** (*BJSM*; 10 cohort studies, all high risk of bias): limited evidence for nine risk factors; the authors name reduced plantar-flexor strength as one of three modifiable ones. 26 other putative factors weren't associated. <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6837257/>
- **Achilles rehab:** loading (eccentric or heavy slow resistance) is the main conservative treatment, and reviews disagree on which kind is best ([PMC10454459](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10454459/)). That's rehab evidence, so it isn't used to justify prevention claims (SA12).
- **Plantar heel pain:** Rathleff 2015's high-load heel raise showed a benefit at 3 months only (n = 48). A larger 2023 trial (Riel, n = 180, seen only through secondary summaries) found little added benefit ([PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/42802)). Not used.
- **Bent and straight knee:** the soleus works most with the knee bent, the gastrocnemius with it straight. That's anatomy (Practice), and it's why SA3 includes both.

### 4. Hips: abductors and adductors

- **Patellofemoral pain rehab:** hip + knee strengthening reduced pain and improved activity more than no training, and more than knee work alone (Nascimento 2018, *JOSPT*; 14 trials). <https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/51308>. One 2021 review found hip-only and knee-only work equivalent ([PubMed 34377715](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34377715/)). Grade for rehab: Moderate.
- **ITB syndrome:** hip abductor strengthening is the most-studied treatment, but the trials are small and mixed (Sanchez-Alvarado 2024, *Front Sports Act Living*; no meta-analysis possible). <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11377285/>
- **Prevention:** hip abductor weakness doesn't predict running injury prospectively. Mucha 2017 found weakness associated with ITB syndrome only cross-sectionally ([PubMed 27693442](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/27693442)). A 2019 review of prospective cohorts found the one signal ran the other way, with very low-quality evidence ([PubMed 30805204](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30805204/)).
- **Adductors:** Harøy 2019 (*BJSM*; cluster RCT, 652 football players): one Copenhagen-adduction exercise, 3×/week pre-season then 1×/week, gave ~41% lower odds of groin problems. Team-sport evidence, not running. <https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/da/publications/the-adductor-strengthening-programme-prevents-groin-problems-amon/>

**Inference:** hip abduction earns a default slot because it's cheap, the catalogue has machine-to-bodyweight versions, and it's the standard rehab exercise for the commonest runner knee complaints. The rule doesn't say it prevents them. Adduction is an option.

### 5. Shins and tibialis

- No trial was found that tests tibialis anterior strengthening for shin pain in runners (search 2026-11-13). One cross-sectional study found deficits in several lower-leg muscles in runners with MTSS. An older review found no prevention method with clear support ([PubMed 18523568](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18523568/)). A 2025 review reports that neuromuscular training and insoles helped ([PubMed 40633262](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40633262/); the abstract wasn't readable when checked, so this is from a search summary only). Grade: Weak. Tibialis raises are an option (SA4), never a default.

### 6. Hamstrings: Nordics

- **van Dyk et al. 2019** (*BJSM*; 15 studies, 8,459 athletes): programmes including Nordics cut hamstring injuries by up to 51% (RR 0.49; RCTs only 0.52). A later reappraisal argues the effect is less certain because Nordics were often combined with other exercises. <https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/21/1362.info>
- **Cuthbert et al. 2020** (*Sports Med*): low-volume Nordic protocols gave eccentric strength gains similar to high-volume ones. A 2025 meta-analysis found that architecture changes needed more volume. <https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1366029/the-effect-of-nordic-hamstring-exercise-intervention-volume-on-eccentric-strength-and-muscle-architecture-adaptations-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analyses>
- Population: sprint-heavy team sports. Distance runners get few hamstring strains, so for Ben it's an option (SA4), and a default only for field and court sports (SA5). Nordics cause marked soreness at first, so SA6 and SA7 apply.

### 7. Single-leg work

- **Liao et al. 2022** (*Biol Sport*; 14 studies, mostly young athletes): unilateral training improved unilateral jumps more (ES 0.89); bilateral training improved bilateral strength more (−0.43); no difference for sprint or change of direction. <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9331349/>. Running is single-leg, so a swap is a reasonable specificity step (Weak/Practice). Making it a swap, not an add, keeps rule 1's volume and the main lifts intact.

### 8. Core

- No systematic review was found showing that core or trunk training improves running economy (search 2026-11-13). The trunk-training reviews found were mostly in team-sport athletes, and individual runner trials disagree (seen through search summaries only). Grade: Weak. Core stays under Ben's existing abs setting, with no running claim (SA11).

### 9. Cycling, swimming, team sports

- **Cycling:** heavy strength training improved cycling efficiency (ES 0.35), anaerobic power and performance, with low certainty (Llanos-Lagos 2025, *Eur J Appl Physiol*; 17 studies, 262 cyclists). <https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-025-05883-2>. Neck and low-back pain are common in cyclists, but no trial of exercise for them in cyclists was found ([Physiopedia, Cyclist's Neck](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Cyclist%27s_Neck), secondary). Upper back and neck work: Practice only, an option.
- **Swimming:** small trials of cuff programmes improved rotation strength ratios, not injury rates (Castro 2025, n = 30, <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11899141/>; Della Tommasina 2023, n = 22, <https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1275285/full>). Weak.
- **Team sports:** FIFA 11+ cut injuries by ~39% (Thorborg 2017, *BJSM*, 4 trials, <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28087568/>). Balance training cuts ankle sprains by about a third across several meta-analyses (e.g. Hübscher 2010, <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19952811/>). Nordics and adductors as in §4 and §6.

### 10. Dose and interference

- The runner trials that failed used low-load bodyweight routines (Wu 2024). The economy gains came from heavy loads or plyometrics over ≥ 6 weeks, 1–4 sessions a week (Llanos-Lagos 2024; Blagrove 2018). Ben's 6-week blocks and 2 leg days fit that.
- Muscle-damaging leg work impairs high-intensity running at 24–48 h and easy running at 48 h (Burt 2023, <https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/77226/>). An acute lower-limb strength session raised soreness and lowered peak torque but didn't affect submaximal running (de Carvalho e Silva 2022, *Sports Med Open*, meta-analysis, <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9385928>). Calf raises and hip abduction are low-damage once familiar; new eccentric work isn't. Hence SA6 and SA7.
- ~6–8 sets a week is Practice: about 2–3 sets per item, in line with the volumes in Harøy 2019 and Cuthbert 2020 and the approved non-priority ranges. It fits rule 1's budget without cutting main lifts.
