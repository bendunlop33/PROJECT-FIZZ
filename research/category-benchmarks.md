# Category Benchmarks — UK Dry Functional Formats

**Author:** CSO · **Date:** 2026-09-22 · **Status:** analysis of panels already read directly; no new sourcing.

Every figure is transcribed from the product's own declared panel as recorded in `research/cube-category-map.md` §2 and `research/competitors.csv`. **FACT** unless marked. Where a brand publishes no numeric panel the row says so rather than estimating.

The point of this cut: a single "how functional is it" score is meaningless across categories. A hydration product and a multivitamin are good at different things, and each needs its own test. So each category below gets **the benchmark that actually decides whether a product is any good at that job**.

---

## 1. Hydration (electrolyte-focused)

**The benchmark: sodium.** Rehydration is driven by sodium, and by sodium–glucose co-transport where carbohydrate is present. Potassium, magnesium and calcium matter, but a hydration product that is light on sodium is not really a hydration product. WHO-spec oral rehydration runs around 1,000 mg sodium per litre; sports hydration typically lands 300–700 mg per serving.

| SIS Hydro | 345 mg / 4 tabs (**~86 mg per tablet**) | 65 mg | 8.1 mg | 102 mg | 269 mg | 0 | Declared per 4 tablets — **per tablet it is the weakest in the set** |
| Phizz Daily 3-in-1 | 320 mg | 115 mg | 56 mg | — | 229 mg | 0 | Solid, and carries a full vitamin load with it |

**THE FINDING, and it is new.** Waterdrop Microlyte declares no sodium figure directly, but it does declare **salt 0.31 g** on the Lemon-Mint and Orange panels. Salt converts to sodium at 0.393, so **sodium ≈ 124 mg per serving** (the same calculation checks out against Applied Nutrition, which declares both: salt 0.69 g and sodium 270 mg).

At ~124 mg, **Microlyte carries roughly a fifth of Liquid I.V.'s sodium and less than half of most of the set — and it declares no potassium at all.** Against the benchmark that actually matters for hydration, Waterdrop's electrolyte product is near the bottom of the category.

**OPINION.** Microlyte is a vitamin cube wearing an electrolyte badge. Its nine vitamins are genuinely strong (§3); its electrolyte profile is not. That reframes the earlier conclusion that "Microlyte already is the product being proposed" — it is the product on vitamins, and it is beatable on hydration. **Anyone entering this format with a real sodium load and a declared potassium figure would out-specify the category leader on the category's own benchmark, in the same cube mass.** Sodium and potassium salts are cheap and light; this is not an A-066 mass problem.

## 2. Energy and caffeine

**The benchmark: is there a stimulant, and at what dose?** Because there is no authorised GB claim for alertness (A-052), everything here is sold on either a declared caffeine figure or a large B-vitamin load. Those are two different products wearing the same word.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Zooki Energy & Focus | **100 mg** | **L-theanine 200 mg** | B12, C, zinc | Stimulant, nootropic-coded |

| Waterdrop Ice Tea Peach | **not declared** | black tea extract 7% | C and niacin at 15% | Ambiguous — see below |
| **Berocca Energy** | **0 mg** | — | B2 971%, B1 945%, B6 507%, C 595% | **Vitamin-energy, no stimulant** |
| H&B High Strength Energy | **0 mg** | — | B12 500 µg, B2 15 mg, C 500 mg | Vitamin-energy, no stimulant |

**THE FINDING.** **The biggest "energy" brand in the UK effervescent category contains no caffeine at all.** Berocca sells energy on B vitamins at 283–971% NRV, and so does Holland & Barrett's own-label. The stimulant half of the category is the smaller half.

That matters directly to the caffeine-free decision. Read with A-052 — caffeine carries no authorised GB claim either — **going caffeine-free puts V-Drop in the same construction as the category's dominant energy brand, not outside the category.** The honest caveat is unchanged: B vitamins do not produce a felt effect, and Berocca's advantage is forty years of brand meaning doing the work that the pharmacology does not.

**A compliance note on Ice Tea Peach.** Waterdrop declares 7% black tea extract and states "caffeine equivalent to a cup of tea" on the collection page, but publishes no caffeine figure on the product page. Retained Reg 1169/2011 requires a caffeine content declaration on drinks above 150 mg/L. **UNVERIFIED whether the prepared drink clears that threshold** — worth a look, because it is the kind of gap that suggests the answer is "just under".

## 3. Everyday vitamins

**The benchmark: coverage, not dose.** Anything can put 1,000% of vitamin C in a tablet. A multivitamin is judged on whether it covers the nutrients people are actually short of. The markers are **vitamin D** (NHS advises supplementation October–March), **folate**, **riboflavin (B2)**, **iodine** and **selenium** — the ones routinely missed.

**THE FINDING.** **No Waterdrop product declares vitamin D, riboflavin, iodine or selenium.** Not one. Microlyte's nine vitamins look impressive until you test them against what a multivitamin is for, at which point it misses all five markers.

And the mass argument does not explain it. Full-NRV riboflavin (1.4 mg), folic acid (200 µg), iodine (150 µg), selenium (55 µg) and vitamin D (5 µg) together weigh **under 10 mg** — negligible against a 2.0 g cube. The bulky minerals at A-066 are a real constraint; these are not.

**Two candidate explanations, and they are testable rather than speculative:** riboflavin fluoresces bright yellow-green in solution, which a brand selling visibly clear, pretty water would reasonably refuse; and vitamin D is fat-soluble, needing a cold-water-soluble encapsulated form that carries real cost and a mass multiplier. Iodine and selenium have no such excuse.

## 4. Functional / targeted supplementation

**The benchmark: is there a hero active beyond vitamins, at a dose the buyer can see?** This is the category Vitamin Well's naming implies and the one the V-Drop question was reaching for.

| Product | Format | Hero active(s) and dose | Claimable in GB? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revive Active | sachet | L-arginine 3,000 mg · D-ribose 3,000 mg · citrulline malate 2,000 mg · AAKG 1,000 mg · taurine 1,000 mg · L-glutamine 500 mg · CoQ10 150 mg | No authorised claim for any of them |
| Zooki Liver Health | sachet | NAC 600 mg · choline 500 mg · silymarin 300 mg | Choline yes, at ≥82.5 mg; NAC and silymarin no |
| Zooki Exercise & Recover | sachet | L-glutamine 1,000 mg · beetroot powder 500 mg | No |
| Zooki Energy & Focus | sachet | caffeine 100 mg · L-theanine 200 mg | No |
| Zooki Skin Radiance | sachet | Damasty® 150 mg (rose and melon extract) | No |
| Coleen Rooney Hydration | sachet | coconut water powder 5,000 mg · dandelion root 250 mg · prickly pear 100 mg | No |
| Vegan Wellness Multivitamin | tablet | ginseng (dose not declared) | On-hold only |
| Berocca Mind | tablet | **Spanish sage essential oil 25 µl** | On-hold only |
| **Every effervescent cube in the survey** | **cube** | **none** | — |

**THE FINDING, and it is the one that ties the whole map together.** Every genuine non-vitamin functional active in the UK dry category lives in a **sachet**. The only exception in a tablet is 25 microlitres of sage oil in Berocca Mind, which is an aroma quantity, not a dose.

**That is a mass story, not a marketing one.** Sachets run 3.7–7 g; cubes run 2.0–2.3 g and are 50–75% acid and carbonate before anything else goes in. A 1,000 mg hero active is routine in a 7 g sachet and arithmetically impossible in a 2 g cube. **The cube format cannot compete in this category, and no amount of positioning changes that** (A-066, A-034).

So the four categories divide cleanly by what the format permits:

- **Hydration** — cube is competitive, and Waterdrop is beatable on the benchmark.
- **Energy** — cube is competitive; caffeine-free is the mainstream construction, not the fringe one.
- **Everyday vitamins** — cube is competitive on coverage, and there is a real, unoccupied gap in the missing markers.
- **Targeted functional** — **the cube is structurally excluded.** Choose a sachet or choose a different category.

## 5. What this changes

1. **The strongest unoccupied position found so far is not "more vitamins than Waterdrop".** It is **a cube that passes its own category's benchmark**: a real sodium and potassium load if hydration, or the five missing coverage markers if everyday vitamins. Both are cheap in mass and neither is what the incumbent does.
2. **Microlyte is beatable on hydration** despite being the strongest cube on vitamins. The earlier "there is no headroom above Microlyte" conclusion was measured on the wrong axis for that category.
3. **Caffeine-free is normal here**, not brave — Berocca, the category's biggest energy brand, is caffeine-free.
4. **The targeted-functional ambition and the cube format are incompatible.** This needs deciding rather than finessing.

## 7. Combinations — the register authorises them, and nobody formulates to them

**The question this answers:** is "a tasty vitamin drink with combinations that actually benefit you" a real differentiator, or just a nicer way of saying more vitamins? It is real, and it is legally sayable.

**FACT — of the 267 GB-authorised claims, 19 describe one nutrient acting on another.** These are not general-function claims; they are claims *about a combination*. Extracted from the parsed register (19 May 2026 spreadsheet):

**The iron system — five interlocking claims**
- *Vitamin C increases iron absorption*
- *Copper contributes to normal iron transport in the body*
- *Riboflavin contributes to the normal metabolism of iron*
- *Vitamin A contributes to normal iron metabolism*
- plus iron's own six claims, including tiredness and fatigue, immune function, oxygen transport and cognitive function

**The homocysteine system — five nutrients, one shared claim**
- *Folate / Vitamin B12 / Vitamin B6 / Betaine / Choline contributes to normal homocysteine metabolism*

**The bone-mineral system**
- *Vitamin D contributes to normal absorption/utilisation of calcium and phosphorus*
- *Vitamin D contributes to normal blood calcium levels*
- with vitamin K's bone claim alongside

**The antioxidant-regeneration pair — the most elegant one in the register**
- *Vitamin C contributes to the regeneration of the reduced form of vitamin E*

**And two singletons:** *Zinc contributes to normal metabolism of vitamin A*; *Magnesium / Vitamin B6 / Zinc contribute to normal protein synthesis*.

### 7.1 Why this matters more than dose

Every claim above is conditioned only on **"source of" — 15% NRV** (A-009). So a combination claim costs no more mass than a single-nutrient one. **The differentiator is which nutrients you put together and why, not how much of each you use** — and unlike dose, that is a design decision rather than a number a competitor can raise in an afternoon.

It also sidesteps the taste ceiling entirely. The gradient at A-076 says taste degrades as dose climbs. A combination claim needs 15% NRV, which sits comfortably below the band where palatability starts to suffer. **Combination and taste are not in tension. Dose and taste are.**

### 7.2 Nobody in the survey has built one

**FACT — checked against all 36 declared panels.** The components are scattered; the architecture is absent.

| Product | Of the 5 iron-system nutrients | Framed as? |
|---|---|---|
| Berocca Immuno | **4** — C 1000 mg, A 700 µg, copper 0.9 mg, **iron 5 mg (36%)** — no riboflavin | Immunity |
| Phizz Daily 3-in-1 | 4 — C, B2, copper, A — **no iron**, so the system does nothing | Hydration |
| Berocca Mind | 3 — C 60 mg, **iron 12 mg (86%)**, B2 1.6 mg — no copper, no A | Focus |
| Vitabiotics Immunace | 3 — no iron | Immunity |
| High5 Zero+ | 3 — no iron | Hydration |
| **Every Waterdrop product** | **0** — no iron, no copper, no riboflavin anywhere in the range | — |

**Berocca Immuno comes closest and does not know it** — it holds four of the five, at 36% NRV iron, and sells the result as immunity. **No product in the UK dry category is designed around, named after, or marketed on a nutrient interaction.**

### 7.3 It fits the cube

**ESTIMATE, from the same salt-mass method as A-038.** A complete iron system at full NRV: iron 14 mg as bisglycinate ≈ 70 mg · vitamin C 80 mg · copper 1 mg as bisglycinate ≈ 10 mg · riboflavin 1.4 mg · vitamin A 800 µg ≈ 5 mg as a CWS powder. **Roughly 170 mg of actives — comfortably inside a 2.0 g cube**, and nothing like the magnesium-and-calcium problem at A-066.

**The one real obstacle is taste, and it is the known one: iron is metallic in an effervescent.** But it is demonstrably solvable — **Berocca Mind carries 12 mg of iron in an effervescent tablet today.** So this is a formulation problem with an existing precedent, not a barrier.

### 7.4 What this changes

1. **The differentiator is combination, not dose, and it is legally sayable.** Nineteen authorised claims describe nutrient interactions; the category uses none of them as an organising idea.
2. **It survives the objection that sank "more vitamins".** Waterdrop can raise a dose cheaply. It cannot cheaply acquire a reason for its formulation, and its current architecture — three vitamins sitting on claim thresholds — is the opposite of a designed combination.
3. **It is compatible with taste**, because 15% NRV sits below the band where the review data shows palatability failing.
4. **The iron system is the strongest candidate** — five interlocking claims, a real UK deficiency behind it, absent from every cube on the market, and it fits the mass budget.

**OPINION, and the caveat that matters.** Nothing here says a customer wants this or would pay for it. It says the claim is lawful, the formulation fits, and the space is empty. Whether "designed to work together" beats "22 nutrients" on a landing page is a demand question this document cannot answer, and the review evidence is silent on it — **nobody asked for combinations either.**

Not "who has more", which §4 of `cube-category-map.md` already settled as roughly the same band. **Which nutrients each brand picks, and whether the picks have a logic.** Waterdrop panels read direct; Vitamin Well from the UK country panels embedded in its product pages. Both 2026-09-22.

| Vitamin C | **100%** | 100% | draw |
| Vitamin A | 20% (Microlyte Berry only) | absent | Waterdrop |
| Thiamine (B1) | **50%** | absent from UK panels | Waterdrop |
| **Riboflavin (B2)** | **absent everywhere** | **absent everywhere** | **neither** |
| Niacin | 50% | 100% | Vitamin Well |
| Pantothenic acid | 50% | 50% | draw |
| **Vitamin B6** | **25–50% in almost every product** | **absent** (except Electrolytes at 20%) | **Waterdrop** |
| Folate | 25% (Microdrink Lemon only) | 50–100% | Vitamin Well |
| Vitamin B12 | 40–50% | 50% | draw |
| Biotin | 50% (Microlyte Berry only) | 100% | Vitamin Well |
| Zinc | 15–20% (Microlyte only) | 45–50% in all | Vitamin Well |
| Selenium | **absent everywhere** | 45% (Reload, Recover) | Vitamin Well |
| Magnesium | 15% | 15% | draw |
| **Iron / copper** | **absent** | **absent** | **neither** |
| Sugar | **0 g** | **21 g** (Original range) | Waterdrop |

### 8.1 The shape of the difference

**Waterdrop is a B-complex-and-vitamin-C brand.** Its picks are almost entirely water-soluble: C, B1, B6, B12, niacin, pantothenic acid. Those are cheap, stable, dissolve clear and need no encapsulation.

**Vitamin Well is a fat-soluble-and-mineral brand.** Vitamin D at 150% in everything, vitamin E at 50–60%, zinc at 45–50%, selenium — and it drops B1 and B6 almost entirely.

**The obvious explanation is format physics, and it is only half right.** Vitamins D and E are fat-soluble and need cold-water-soluble encapsulated forms — trivial to disperse through 500 ml of bottled liquid, hard in a 2 g cube that has to dissolve clear. **But Microlyte Berry carries vitamin A at 20% and vitamin E at 30%, both fat-soluble.** So the cube *can* take them. **Waterdrop's total absence of vitamin D is therefore a choice, not a constraint** — and it is the single largest formulation gap between the two brands.

**One shared blind spot, and it is conspicuous: neither brand carries riboflavin, iron or copper.** Not in any product, in either range. Riboflavin's bright yellow-green fluorescence is a plausible reason for a brand selling clear pretty water; iron's metallic note is a plausible reason for both. Neither explanation covers copper.

### 8.2 Under the combination lens (§7), both are accidental

Neither brand's panel shows any sign of being designed around a nutrient interaction. But two of the register's authorised combinations turn up in their products **by accident**:

**The C + E regeneration pair** — *"Vitamin C contributes to the regeneration of the reduced form of vitamin E"*. **Both brands hold it**: Microlyte Berry at C 100% / E 30%, Vitamin Well Hydrate at C 100% / E 50%. Neither, on the pages read, says a word about it.

**The homocysteine trio — and this one is the better illustration.** The claim needs folate, B12 or B6, each at 15% NRV. **Waterdrop Microdrink LEMON carries all three — folic acid 25%, B12 25%, B6 25% — a complete, authorised, three-nutrient combination sitting in a 2.1 g cube on sale today.** Vitamin Well's UK panels carry folate and B12 but no B6, so they cannot complete it.

**Waterdrop sells that product as "Lemon."**

**OPINION.** That is the clearest evidence in the project that combination is unoccupied territory rather than a crowded one. The strongest combination on either brand's shelf is an accident of a flavour SKU, and the brand that owns it has not noticed. **The gap is not that nobody can build these. It is that nobody is thinking in these terms at all.**

**Antioxidant** is not the chassis at all. It drops D and the entire B-complex and carries **vitamin E 100%, vitamin C 100%, zinc 50%, selenium 100%, manganese 40%**. **Defence** is the second: D 150% + C 100% + folate 100% + zinc 50%, all four of which carry authorised immune-function claims. Four of the ten nutrients in the authorised immune set.

### 9.3 The gap, stated precisely

Four things are true at once, each evidenced above or in §7:

2. **The best liquid attempts are not sold here.** Vitamin Well's Antioxidant and Defence have no UK panel.
3. **Even those attempts are incomplete.** Antioxidant holds five of the seven authorised antioxidant nutrients. **The two it is missing are copper and riboflavin** — which are, precisely, the two nutrients absent from Waterdrop's and Vitamin Well's entire ranges (§8.1). **The complete authorised antioxidant combination has never been built by anyone in this category.**

**So the gap is not "a more functional Waterdrop", and not "Vitamin Well in a cube". It is: the first product in the UK designed around a complete authorised nutrient combination, in a format that tastes good enough to drink daily.**

**OPINION, and the limit of it.** This says the claims are lawful, the sets are incomplete, the formats permit it and the space is empty. It does not say a buyer wants it. Nobody in the review data asked for combinations, and "designed to work together" versus "22 nutrients" is still an untested proposition.

## 10. Designing the hydration cube — what to put in, and what the competitors actually use

### 10.1 Yes to all three, and in an effervescent two of them are nearly free

**The key structural point: in an effervescent, the carbonate half of the acid–carbonate couple can *be* the electrolyte source.** Waterdrop's own Microlyte ingredient list reads *"acidifier: citric acid; **potassium chloride**, acidity regulator: calcium phosphates; **magnesium carbonate, sodium carbonate**…"* — the fizz chemistry and the mineral content are the same ingredients.

**ESTIMATE** (stoichiometry, not a formulation — a formulator sets the real figures):

| Salt | Delivers | In a 2.0 g cube |
|---|---|---|
| Potassium bicarbonate (39.1% K) | ~500 mg → **195 mg potassium** | part of the carbonate base, so no extra mass |
| Sodium bicarbonate (27.4% Na) | ~400 mg → **110 mg sodium** | likewise |
| Potassium chloride (52.4% K, 47.6% Cl) | ~200 mg → **105 mg K + 95 mg chloride** | small addition |
| Magnesium carbonate (28.8% Mg) | ~200 mg → **58 mg magnesium** | doubles as an acidity regulator |

**Running total: potassium ~300 mg, magnesium ~58 mg, sodium ~110 mg, chloride ~95 mg** — inside a 2.0 g cube that still has ~700 mg of citric acid and room for flavour.

### 10.2 The claims arithmetic makes potassium and magnesium the priority, not sodium

| Nutrient | 15% NRV | Authorised claims it unlocks |
|---|---|---|
| **Magnesium** | **56 mg** | **TEN** — electrolyte balance, muscle function, nervous system, tiredness and fatigue, energy metabolism, psychological function, protein synthesis, bones, teeth, cell division |
| **Potassium** | **300 mg** | **THREE** — nervous system, muscle function, **maintenance of normal blood pressure** |
| Chloride | 120 mg | one — digestion via stomach acid |
| Calcium | 120 mg | ten, but mostly bone claims |
| **Sodium** | **no NRV** | **NONE. Sodium carries no authorised claim at all.** |

**Magnesium at 56 mg is the highest claim-per-milligram nutrient in the entire register — ten claims for 56 mg.** Potassium at 300 mg is heavy but buys three, including blood pressure, which no cube in the UK currently claims.

**And sodium — the nutrient the sports category is built on — buys nothing.** It has no NRV and no authorised claim. It is an efficacy ingredient only.

### 10.3 The honest constraint on sodium, and why it argues for the user's framing

**A 2.0 g cube cannot be a serious sports-rehydration product.** Sports hydration runs 300–700 mg sodium per serving; 300 mg of sodium needs ~770 mg of sodium chloride or ~1,100 mg of bicarbonate, and with ~700 mg of citric acid already committed there is no room for that plus potassium plus magnesium plus flavour. **Microlyte's ~63–124 mg is not laziness. It is the format.**

**But for an everyday product that is the right answer anyway, and NDNS says so.** The report records *"a significant average yearly reduction in sodium intake in all age/sex groups"* — reported as progress, because reducing UK salt intake is public health policy. **Sodium is the one mineral in this category that Britons consume too much of, not too little.** Potassium and magnesium are the under-consumed ones — NDNS records low magnesium intakes across all adult groups.

**OPINION, and it resolves the SKU: an everyday hydration cube should be potassium- and magnesium-led with modest sodium.** That is nutritionally correct for a well person, it is what fits in 2.0 g, and it is a genuinely different product from the sports-hydration shelf rather than a weaker copy of it. The user's instinct — *"everyday hydration with different salts"* — is the right read.

### 10.5 The two reference brands both fail this on their own terms

**FACT — Vitamin Well Hydrate declares magnesium and nothing else.** No sodium, no potassium, no chloride, no calcium. The product called *Hydrate* carries **one** electrolyte. Vitamin Well's actual electrolyte product is a separate SKU, Electrolytes Isotonic, at magnesium 3% NRV and potassium 3%.

**FACT — Waterdrop Microlyte declares magnesium, calcium, chloride and zinc, and no potassium — despite potassium chloride being its second ingredient.**

**So on the three minerals that matter for everyday hydration, neither reference brand declares all three, and one declares only one of them.** A cube that simply declares potassium at 300 mg, magnesium at 58 mg and sodium honestly would out-specify both — and, unlike the sodium race, it is achievable in the format.
