## 1. The legal framework — five rules that decide everything

### 1.1 The test is consumption in the EU before 15 May 1997
A food is novel in GB if it was not used for human consumption to a significant degree within the EU before 15 May 1997. If it is novel, it cannot be sold without a pre-market authorisation. **FACT, MED confidence** — this is settled law, repeated consistently across every source. (Paraphrased; I could not read the FSA guidance page directly: https://www.food.gov.uk/business-guidance/regulated-products/novel-foods-guidance)

### 1.2 The EU Novel Food Catalogue is indicative only, and GB has diverged
Two separate points, both important, and the team has been conflating them:

- The Catalogue is **non-binding and non-exhaustive**. It is an indicative record of history-of-use information, not a legal list. Absence from it proves nothing either way.
- **EU novel-food status decisions made before 1 January 2021 continue to apply in GB. EU decisions after that date do not apply in GB**, though the FSA may use them as evidence of history of consumption.

**So: where I cite the EU catalogue below, I am reasoning from an EU source about a GB position.** That is legitimate for pre-2021 entries and for the underlying consumption evidence, but it is not a GB determination. The burden sits on the food business operator to make and defend its own assessment.
**FACT, MED.** Sources (read via search summaries): [Ashbury Global](https://ashbury.global/blog/what-are-the-rules-for-novel-foods-in-the-uk/); [Fieldfisher on FSA novel foods](https://www.fieldfisher.com/en/insights/novel-foods-and-the-food-standards-agency-a-vision-for-the-future-of-market-authorisations); [Finnish Food Authority on the catalogue](https://www.ruokavirasto.fi/en/foodstuffs/food-sector/food-ingredients-and-contents/novel-foods/establishing-novel-food-status-of-a-food/novel-food-catalogue-of-european-commission/)

### 1.3 Status attaches to a species-and-form pair, not to a species
This is the crux of the task and the CSO's original error in reverse. A catalogue entry reads, in effect, "*Hericium erinaceus*, fruiting body — not novel" and, separately, "*Hericium erinaceus*, dehydrated mycelium powder — novel". **The same mushroom holds two opposite statuses at once.** Four forms must be assessed separately:

| Form | General position |
|---|---|
| **(a) Whole / powdered fruiting body** | Takes the species' history-of-use status directly. This is the safest form for every species. |
| **(b) Non-concentrated aqueous (hot water) extract** | By common agreement between the Commission and Member States, a non-concentrated water extract of a non-novel food **takes the same status as the food**. Safe, *if* genuinely non-concentrated. |
| **(c) Concentrated extract (10:1, 20:1)** | **Not covered by rule (b).** A 10:1 or 20:1 extract is by definition concentrated. Concentration can alter the constituent profile in a way that has no consumption history, so the extract can be novel even though the mushroom is not. **This is an independent risk that applies to every species in the table, including the "green" ones.** |
| **(d) Mycelium, incl. mycelium-on-grain** | **Dehydrated mycelium powder is an unauthorised novel food for essentially every functional species I checked** — lion's mane, reishi, shiitake, tremella, *Agaricus subrufescens*. Mycelium-on-grain is excluded outright. |

And a fifth axis: **solvent**. Catalogue entries where the extract limb is listed specify an **aqueous** extract. **Ethanolic and dual (water + ethanol) extracts are not covered** by those entries. A "dual-extracted" mushroom — the standard premium-positioning phrase in this category — is therefore in unassessed territory.
**FACT on the doctrine, MED. The per-species application is LOW-to-MED, see §2.** Sources: [functionalmushrooms.eu species tracker](https://functionalmushrooms.eu/novel-food-and-functional-mushrooms/); [Hooley Brown, from chaga to cordyceps](https://www.hooleybrown.com/blog-post/functional-mushroom-novel-food-rules-explained); [EU consult-status, lion's mane extract powder](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/1db081b3-c497-4349-85f9-5347f2989ca8_en?filename=novel-food_consult-status_lions-mane-extract-powder.pdf); [EU consult-status, *H. erinaceus* dehydrated mycelium (AESAN)](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b075e41c-cccb-46ae-81c2-3a86fb3c390f_en?filename=novel-food_consult-status_hericium-erinaceus_aesan.pdf); [EU consult-status, *Ganoderma lucidum* dehydrated mycelium (AESAN)](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/1757ffec-0570-40ec-9c63-10c6661e6c3f_en?filename=novel-food_consult-status_ganoderma-lucidum_aesan.pdf)

### 1.4 "Not novel **in food supplements**" is narrower than "not novel"
Several entries — **chaga most clearly** — record that the pre-1997 consumption was *in food supplements*, and that the same material is **novel for any other food application**. A mushroom you may lawfully put in a capsule may be an unauthorised novel food in a beverage.

**This lands directly on V-Drop.** A "food supplement" is defined as a food intended to supplement the normal diet, a concentrated source of nutrients or other substances with a nutritional or physiological effect, **sold in dose form**; "dose form" covers capsules, pastilles, tablets, pills, sachets of powder and similar forms designed to be taken in measured small unit quantities.

An effervescent **tablet** is prima facie a dose form. But V-Drop's tablet is designed to be dissolved into 500 ml of water and drunk as a flavoured beverage, and the brand proposition is explicitly a *drink*. **Whether the finished product is legally a food supplement or a beverage is an open and load-bearing question** — it decides whether the supplements-only limb of the chaga entry (and any similar entries) is available. I have not resolved it and it is not safe to assume the answer.
**FACT on the definition / OPINION on the application to V-Drop, MED.** Sources: [Food Supplements (England) Regulations 2003](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2003/1387); [gov.uk guidance notes on the food supplements legislation](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/food-supplements-guidance-and-faqs/guidance-notes-on-legislation-implementing-directive-200246ec-on-food-supplements); [EU consult-status, chaga extract powder](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/3b97b6ad-ff84-4153-a3e5-4abc518a0273_en?filename=novel-food_consult-status_chaga-extract-powder.pdf)

### 1.5 The FSA enforces species-specifically, and it enforces through local authorities
The December 2025 action was **not** a mushroom ban. It named **two species**: *Trametes versicolor* (turkey tail) and *Cordyceps militaris*. It reached businesses via their **local council acting on the FSA's behalf**. It was a regulatory classification action, not a safety action — the FSA has not alleged harm. See §3 for what follows from the enforcement route.

### 3.2 On the dose figure (A-040)
A search summary of the Amazon UK listing page states: **"The mushroom extracts include 250mg each of: Lion's Mane, Cordyceps, Chaga, Maitake, Shiitake, Reishi, Tremella, Royal Sun, Black Fungus, and White Button"**, alongside an ingredient list of glucose syrup, sugar, glucose, pectin, citric acid, natural raspberry flavour, sodium citrate, fruit and vegetable juice concentrate and a glazing agent of sunflower oil and carnauba wax.

**This is corroboration of the 250 mg × 10 = 2,500 mg figure from a second, non-affiliate source** (the marketplace listing, read via search summary, rather than `topshelfmushrooms.com`). It is **not** resolution. Two problems survive intact:

1. **The CSO's physical-implausibility argument still stands and is not answered.** 2,500 mg per 2-gummy serving is 1,250 mg per gummy. The ingredient list is led by glucose syrup, sugar and glucose, with pectin as the gelling agent. A gummy of typical 2.5–4 g mass carrying 1,250 mg of bitter mushroom extract implies a 31–50% active load in a sugar-pectin matrix. That is a strong prior against the figure being declared extract mass.
2. **The raw-equivalent trap is not excluded.** A-040 records the product as "stated 10:1". Listing text reading "250 mg each" is exactly what a 10:1 raw-equivalent marketing figure looks like when compressed into a bullet point. Only the formatted supplement facts panel distinguishes "250 mg extract" from "250 mg (equivalent to 2,500 mg dried mushroom)".

**Verdict on A-040's dose: remains UNVERIFIED. Corroborated once, not resolved. Do not restore the "5.7× better price-to-potency" conclusion.**

### 3.4 Why (d) is the probable answer, and why it matters more than the answer
The December 2025 enforcement reached Bristol Fungarium **through its local council, acting on the FSA's behalf**. That is the standard UK food enforcement route, and it has a structural property: **it works on food business operators with a UK establishment and a home local authority. It works poorly or not at all on an overseas seller shipping into the UK through a marketplace.** Ankhway operates a `.com` brand site plus a US mirror (`ankhwaylife.com`) and sells through Amazon UK and eBay UK. A-040 notes one review source attributing it to "ATC MN LTD", unverified.

There is a second, independent compliance problem for Ankhway that has gone unremarked: **if the product genuinely uses 10:1 concentrated extracts, then under §1.3(c) even its nine non-cordyceps species are in unassessed concentrated-extract territory.** Concentration status is not cured by the species being non-novel. On that reading Ankhway is non-compliant twice over.

**The strategic lesson is the important output here, and it cuts against V-Drop, not for it:**

> V-Drop would be a **UK-domiciled food business operator with a registered establishment, a home local authority, D2C infrastructure and a brand worth suing**. Ankhway is an overseas marketplace seller. **The two face completely different enforcement probabilities for identical conduct.** Benchmarking V-Drop's regulatory risk appetite against what an Amazon marketplace seller gets away with is a category error, and it is the sort of error that ends a launch. Bristol Fungarium — a small, legitimate, UK-registered, organic, UK-growing producer — is precisely the profile that got the knock on the door.

**OPINION, reasoned from the sourced enforcement route. Confidence MED.** Note also that even if Ankhway is non-compliant, it remains a **commercial** benchmark for the user's positioning reference; the competitive-threat conclusion in A-040 is separate from the compliance conclusion here.

## 4. The claims position — stated plainly

### 4.1 The CSO's understanding is CORRECT, and if anything understated

**No mushroom species carries any authorised GB health claim. Not one, for any of the twelve species in §2, in any form, at any dose.** Nothing about focus, clarity, memory, cognition, immunity, energy, stamina, stress, mood, gut health, sleep, skin or recovery may be said about a mushroom in GB.

This is confirmed on three independent legs:

1. **Nothing in the register.** Only claims on the GB NHC register may be used. My §5 audit in `claims-library.md` found zero authorised claims for lion's mane and cordyceps; extending the search across shiitake, maitake, reishi, chaga, tremella, royal sun, black fungus and white button returns the same answer. (https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/great-britain-nutrition-and-health-claims-nhc-register)
2. **The beta-glucan trap is closed.** There *are* authorised beta-glucan claims — but they are conditioned on **oat and barley** beta-glucan at ≥3 g/day for the cholesterol claims. **Mushroom beta-(1-3,1-6)-D-glucan is a structurally different molecule and is not covered.** A product cannot borrow the oat claim for a mushroom ingredient. This is a live temptation in this category and it is unlawful. **FACT, MED.** ([EU claims register](https://ec.europa.eu/food/food-feed-portal/backend/claims/files/euregister.pdf); [EFSA oat beta-glucan opinion](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1885))
3. **The ASA enforces it, hard and repeatedly.** See §4.2.

### 4.2 What the ASA has actually ruled in this space
The ASA has been actively monitoring this category and the rulings are consistent and adverse:

- **24 July 2024 — six upheld rulings on food supplement claims published on a single day**, including **Ejec Ventures LLC t/a Auri Nutrition** ("nootropic super mushroom gummies") and **Well Gummies**. The ASA held that claims implying that gummies containing turkey tail, cordyceps, lion's mane, chaga and reishi had beneficial health effects were **specific health claims** for which no authorisation existed on the GB register.
- **"Nootropic" is itself a specific health claim**, even though many consumers do not know the word. It is unusable.
- **DIRTEA** removed advertising following an ASA ruling (2023).
- **Birling Shore Ltd t/a ShroomIQ** (2025) and **Vitality Greens** (2024) — further upheld rulings in the same territory.
- Claims about anxiety, dementia, ADHD, Alzheimer's, hormonal issues, skin conditions and flu defence were held likely to be read as **medical claims** — a more serious breach than an unauthorised health claim, and one that can put the product into the medicines regime.
- The CAP advice article for this category is titled *Shroom for improvement? Navigating the advertising rules for functional mushrooms*.

### 4.3 The general-health-claim trap — the part most brands get wrong
A non-specific, general wellbeing claim ("supports your best day", "for a clearer head") is permitted **only if it is accompanied by a relevant, specific, authorised health claim**. The ASA states this explicitly for functional mushroom ads.

**Since no mushroom claim is authorised, a mushroom can never supply the accompanying claim.** The accompanying claim must come from an added vitamin or mineral. That creates a second problem: if the pack fronts a mushroom and the compliance is carried by a vitamin, the ASA may find that the overall impression attributes the benefit to the mushroom. **Juxtaposing "Lion's Mane" with "Pantothenic acid contributes to normal mental performance" risks being read as a lion's mane cognition claim by implication.** Implied claims are caught. This is a real and under-appreciated design constraint.

### 4.4 On-hold claims for mushrooms
On-hold Article 13(1) claims **may** still be used in GB under the transitional arrangement (unlike the EU post-*Novel Nutriology*, C-386/23, 30 April 2025). The DHSC publishes the spreadsheet. Around 1,548 botanical claims sit on hold, and the list **does include fungal entries** — *Ganoderma lucidum* (immune-system relationships) and *Lentinula edodes* appear in the underlying Article 13 consolidated list.

**But the conditions are severe, and for a premium D2C brand I would not use this route:**
- The claim must match the entry on **all** of: plant/fungus part, wording, health relationship, and proposed conditions of use **including dose**. Any deviation and it is simply an unauthorised health claim.
- The marketer must hold the substantiation.
- The claim territory is immune function and psychological function — precisely the territory the CJEU singled out.
- **A GB/EU realignment on claims is expected around mid-2027**, i.e. inside the life of a launch.
- The ASA rulings in §4.2 involved exactly these species and the on-hold route did not save any of them.

**Verdict: 🟠 technically available, practically unusable. Do not build a proposition on it.**
Sources: [Mills & Reeve on the DH spreadsheet](https://www.mills-reeve.com/blogs/food-and-agribusiness/june-2014/department-of-health-provides-spreadsheet-of-artic/); [Mills & Reeve on the EU challenge](https://www.mills-reeve.com/publications/on-hold-botanical-claims-eu-challenge/); [EFSA consolidated Article 13 list](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/topic/ndaart13ref02.pdf)

### 4.5 "Traditional use" framing — correcting a common misconception
**There is no "traditional use" claims route for a food supplement in GB.** The Traditional Herbal Registration scheme is a **medicines** route operated by the MHRA; a product with a THR is a licensed medicine, not a food, and the two regimes are mutually exclusive for a given product. Putting "traditionally used in East Asia to support immunity" on a food supplement is an unauthorised health claim and, depending on wording, a medicinal claim. **It is not a safe harbour. It is a worse position than saying nothing.** **OPINION with high confidence on the structure; consultant to confirm — see §8 Q7.**

### 4.6 What a compliant GB mushroom product CAN say

**🟢 Can say — factual, non-health statements**
- **Name and quantify the ingredient.** "Organic shiitake (*Lentinula edodes*) fruiting body extract — 500 mg." This is mandatory anyway, and it is the whole of what you get to say about the mushroom.
- **Composition and provenance facts:** "10 mushrooms", "100% fruiting body, no mycelium-on-grain, no added starch", "organic", "UK grown", "vegan", "hot-water extracted".
- **Analytical facts, carefully:** "standardised to 30% beta-glucans", "beta-glucans tested, not guessed". These are compositional statements, not health claims — **provided nothing on pack, in the copy around them, or in the imagery implies a benefit from the beta-glucan.** The moment the number is presented as *why it is good for you*, it becomes a health claim. 🟠
- **Sensory and format claims:** taste, dissolves clear, no earthy aftertaste, one tablet, no scoop. **This is the one territory where V-Drop's differentiator (taste and ease, per the 2026-09-22 decision) is fully legal and fully ownable.**
- **Free-from claims:** sugar-free, no artificial colours (subject to the conditions for those claims, and note the A-039 finding that "natural / minimally processed" is *not* available for an effervescent).

**🟠 With care**
- A general wellbeing claim, **only** alongside an authorised specific claim from an added vitamin or mineral, and only if the overall impression does not attribute the benefit to the mushroom (§4.3).
- Consumer testimonials and influencer content: **the ASA treats these as the advertiser's own claims.** A creator saying "my focus is unreal" is your unauthorised health claim.
- Product and variant **names**. A SKU called "Focus" or "Clarity" is capable of being a health claim in itself. High risk.

**🔴 Cannot say**
- Anything about focus, clarity, concentration, memory, brain, cognition, mental performance, immunity, immune support, defence, energy, stamina, endurance, recovery, stress, calm, mood, gut, sleep or skin **attributable to a mushroom**.
- "Nootropic", "adaptogen", "functional mushroom" used as a benefit rather than a descriptor, "medicinal mushroom" (medicinal claim), "superfood" as a health claim.
- Any raw-equivalent potency framing that implies efficacy ("4,000 mg raw equivalent") — see A-031; and for a concentrated extract the framing also advertises the novel food problem.
- Disease, symptom or body-part-condition references of any kind.

### 4.7 The one genuine route to a claimable mushroom ingredient
**UV-treated *Agaricus bisporus* mushroom powder with increased vitamin D2** is the only mechanism I found by which a mushroom ingredient can lawfully carry a health claim in GB. The claim is not a mushroom claim — it is a **vitamin D** claim, delivered by a mushroom-derived nutrient source. The authorised vitamin D claims include, in register wording:

> "Vitamin D contributes to the normal function of the immune system"
> "Vitamin D contributes to the maintenance of normal bones"
> "Vitamin D contributes to the maintenance of normal muscle function"

**These three strings are quoted from recollection of retained Reg (EU) 432/2012 and MUST be re-verified character-for-character against the GB NHC register spreadsheet before use — I could not download it (§0). Treat as UNVERIFIED wording.** All are conditioned on "source of vitamin D" = 15% NRV; NRV for vitamin D is 5 µg, so the threshold is **0.75 µg per portion** — trivially met.

**This is the single most commercially useful finding in this document**, and it is the only bridge between a mushroom hero and the "clinical" positioning (decision of 2026-09-22). **Three caveats, all material:** (i) GB authorisation status of the novel food is **UNVERIFIED** — assessed is not authorised, and A-010 is the cautionary tale; (ii) authorisations can be **applicant-specific and data-protected** for five years, which may mean buying from a named supplier or not at all; (iii) the reported beverage condition is **1.125 µg per 100 ml**, i.e. ~5.6 µg in a 500 ml drink — which clears the claim threshold comfortably but again turns on whether V-Drop is a supplement or a beverage (§1.4).

## 5. Verdict — is a compliant, claimable mushroom product possible in GB today?

### 5.1 Compliant: **YES, clearly.** The dead-end advice was wrong.

It would have to look like this:

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Species** | Shiitake, maitake, reishi, chaga, tremella, royal sun (*A. subrufescens*), lion's mane, white button, black fungus. **Shiitake, white button and lion's mane are the most defensible**; shiitake especially, being an ordinary EU food. |
| **Excluded outright** | ***Cordyceps militaris*** and **turkey tail** — FSA stop-sale in force. |
| **Handle with care** | *Cordyceps sinensis* / CS-4 — probably permitted in a **food supplement**, but the label must name the binomial, and the supplements-only limb is unresolved for a drink. |
| **Form** | **Whole or powdered fruiting body, or a non-concentrated hot-water extract.** Nothing else. |
| **Forbidden forms** | **Mycelium and mycelium-on-grain** (novel for essentially every functional species). **Dual / ethanolic extracts** (outside the catalogue entries). **Concentrated extracts at any ratio** (10:1, 20:1 — unassessed, and this applies even to the green-rated species). |
| **Legal category** | It must be, and must be presented as, a **food supplement in dose form**. Get this decided before formulation, because chaga in particular depends on it. |
| **Documentation** | A written novel-food status opinion for the **exact** specification of every species and form, held on file, plus supplier certificates of analysis confirming species identity, fruiting-body-only sourcing and extraction solvent. |

### 5.2 Claimable: **NO — not about the mushrooms.**

A perfectly legal shiitake / maitake / reishi blend **can say nothing whatsoever about focus, clarity, immunity, energy or recovery.** The CSO's understanding is correct and should not be softened. The only claims available to such a product come from **added vitamins and minerals**, or from **UV vitamin D2 mushroom powder** (§4.7, subject to its own caveats).

### 5.3 The strategic collision — this is the finding that matters

The positioning agreed on 2026-09-22 is **"clinical but tasty and cool"**. Set that against §4:

- **"Clinical" requires the product to assert a substantiated effect.** A mushroom hero cannot assert any effect. It cannot even be named alongside a vitamin claim without implication risk (§4.3).
- So a mushroom-led V-Drop would be **a premium-priced product whose hero ingredient is legally mute**, with the entire claim burden carried by vitamins that cost pennies and are equally available to a £0.06/serving Tesco effervescent (A-009).
- **That is not "clinical". It is "vibes at £0.83 a serving"** — and it is exactly the proposition the ASA has upheld six rulings against in this category.

**Two coherent ways out, and they are genuinely different propositions:**

**Option A — Mushrooms as a taste-and-ritual asset, not a claim asset.** Lean entirely on the 2026-09-22 decision that the differentiator is **taste and ease**. Mushrooms are named, quantified, provenance-led and *silent*. All legal weight sits on added vitamins and minerals. Honest, compliant, and it plays to the one territory that is fully ownable. **But it forfeits "clinical", and it collides with A-034 — no effervescent tablet carrying a gram-scale mushroom extract was found anywhere, and taste-masking a bitter botanical carries a ~3× mass penalty. The taste differentiator and the mushroom hero are fighting each other in the same tablet.**

**Option B — Vitamin-led, mushroom-sourced.** UV *A. bisporus* vitamin D2 as a genuine hero: a mushroom-derived nutrient that carries real authorised claims including immune function. Add a small, honest fruiting-body blend for the story. This is the only build where "mushroom" and "clinical" can coexist lawfully. **Contingent on the GB authorisation actually existing and being accessible (§4.7).**

**And the standing counter-argument has not gone away:** A-026 records that **78% of Britons have heard little or nothing about functional mushrooms and only 3% have tried them**. A hero ingredient that is unknown to the market *and* legally mute is a hard thing to charge a premium for. The regulatory position is better than the CSO said. It is not good.
