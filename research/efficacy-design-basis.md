# Efficacy Design Basis — what a cube would need to actually do something

**Author:** CSO · **Date:** 2026-09-22
**Why this exists:** the user's correction of 2026-09-22 — *"it's not about creating a product others don't have, but rather a product that truly works and delivers what you need."* Every prior cut in this project selected nutrients by **market scarcity**. This one selects them by **evidenced UK shortfall**. The two lists overlap but they are not the same, and where they differ the efficacy list should win.

Primary source throughout: **NDNS Rolling Programme Years 1–9 (2008/09–2016/17), §3.4 Micronutrients**, read directly from the report PDF on 2026-09-22 (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5c45e22340f0b61704aec504/NDNS_UK_Y1-9_report.pdf). NDNS intakes **exclude supplements**, which is the right basis for designing one.

---

## 1. The correction this forces, and it undoes advice I gave twice

**I have twice argued that combination claims are "free", because every authorised combination claim needs only 15% NRV and 15% sits below the taste ceiling.**

**On an efficacy test that is the wrong design target.** 15% of a reference value is the legal minimum at which a nutrient may be *named in a claim*. It is not a dose that corrects a shortfall, and it is not what "truly works" means. A product designed to 15% NRV is designed to be sayable, not to be effective — which is precisely the criticism this project has levelled at Waterdrop's dosing ladder.

**The efficacy design target is the RNI — around 100% NRV — for the nutrients people are actually short of.** Not 15%, which does nothing. Not 1,000%, which is excreted and is theatre. **FACT-adjacent OPINION: the category's two extremes are both wrong, and the honest middle is unoccupied.**

## 3. Does the format permit efficacy? Mostly yes — with one real failure

Doses at **100% NRV**, with approximate salt masses (ESTIMATE, from the method at A-038; a formulator sets the real figures):

| Nutrient | 100% NRV | Approx. mass as a usable form | Fits a 2.0 g cube? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vitamin D | 5 µg | ~25 mg as a cold-water-soluble powder | **Yes** |
| Iodine | 150 µg | ~0.2 mg as potassium iodide | **Yes, trivially** |
| Selenium | 55 µg | ~0.14 mg as sodium selenite | **Yes, trivially** |
| Folate | 200 µg | ~0.2 mg | **Yes, trivially** |
| Iron | 14 mg | ~70 mg as bisglycinate | **Yes** |
| Vitamin C | 80 mg | 80 mg | **Yes** |
| Zinc | 10 mg | ~50 mg as bisglycinate | **Yes** |
| **Magnesium** | **375 mg** | **~1,900 mg as citrate** | **NO. Not at any plausible cube size.** |

**The finding: every evidenced UK shortfall except magnesium can be delivered at a full 100% NRV inside a 2.0 g cube, with room to spare.** Together, D + iodine + selenium + folate + iron + C + zinc come to roughly **230 mg** of actives.

**Magnesium is the honest failure and it should not be papered over.** It is a real, evidenced, all-adult shortfall, and the format cannot carry a meaningful dose — the whole category tops out at 56 mg (15%), including Waterdrop, Phizz and Vitamin Well, and High5's 150 mg (40%) in a 7 g sachet is the ceiling anywhere. **A cube can be a source of magnesium. It cannot be a solution to a magnesium shortfall, and the brand should not imply otherwise.**

## 4. Two efficacy levers nobody in the category uses

**Source form.** Bioavailability is an efficacy variable, and A-043 established by direct read of Schedule 2 that GB permits the good forms — **zinc bisglycinate, ferrous bisglycinate, copper bisglycinate, magnesium bisglycinate, methylcobalamin, pyridoxal-5′-phosphate, calcium-L-methylfolate** are all listed. The declared ingredient lists in `research/competitors.csv` show the category using the cheap ones instead: **zinc oxide, cyanocobalamin, ferrous lactate, pyridoxine hydrochloride.** **OPINION: this is a real, legal, unused efficacy lever and it is invisible on a front-of-pack number — which is exactly why nobody bothers.**

**Absorption pairing.** *Vitamin C increases iron absorption* is not only an authorised claim, it is a mechanism. Same for *vitamin D contributes to normal absorption/utilisation of calcium*. Pairing on this basis is efficacy-led and happens to be claimable — the right way round, rather than picking a claim and hoping.

## 5. What an efficacy-led cube looks like

**Design rules, in order:**
1. **Select on evidenced UK shortfall, not market scarcity.** Vitamin D first — it is the only universal one.
2. **Dose to ~100% NRV of those nutrients.** Not 15%, which is a permission to speak. Not 1,000%, which is excreted.
3. **Use the bioavailable permitted source form** where it materially changes absorption.
4. **Pair on real mechanisms** — C with iron, D with its dependents.
5. **Do not pad.** Every nutrient Britons already get enough of — riboflavin, calcium, vitamin A, vitamin C beyond 100% — is mass, cost and taste spent on nothing, and dilutes the argument that the product is designed rather than assembled.
6. **Be honest where the format cannot deliver** — magnesium above all.

**On that basis the efficacy core is: vitamin D, iodine, selenium, folate, iron, vitamin C, zinc — about 230 mg, all at 100% NRV, comfortably inside a 2.0 g cube.**

**And the claims come free rather than being designed for:** that set carries the complete authorised **immune** claim set bar copper, the complete **cognitive** set (iodine + iron + zinc), the **iron absorption** pair, and the tiredness-and-fatigue claim on four separate nutrients. **Design for efficacy and the claims follow. Design for claims and you get a 15% NRV product that says a lot and does little** — which is a fair description of most of the category.

## 6. What is still not evidenced

- **Nobody asked for this.** The review mining found no demand for combinations or for higher dosing; the largest complaint is flavour intensity. This document says what would work, not what would sell.
- **Selenium rests on a secondary source.** Verify the 50.3% figure against NDNS before it is used anywhere customer-facing.
- **Doses here are CSO arithmetic, not formulation.** Salt masses, overages, stability and interactions are a formulator's job. Iron's metallic note in an effervescent is the known risk and remains unsolved.
- **NDNS Y1–9 covers 2008/09–2016/17.** A more recent wave may have shifted these; the direction of travel it reports is downward, but the figures should be refreshed before they anchor anything final.
